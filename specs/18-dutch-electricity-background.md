# Background — Dutch electricity metering, billing and pricing

> **Purpose:** the domain background the rest of this package assumes. Why the meter
> cannot tell you your consumption, how a kWh's price is built up, what changes on
> 1 January 2027, and which of the 2027 numbers are law and which are guesses.
> **Audience:** everyone, and non-Dutch implementers in particular. Read this before
> the pricing and ingest algorithms if the Dutch regime is unfamiliar.
> **Read with:** [10-pricing.md](10-pricing.md), which implements Parts E2 and E5;
> [09-ingest-algorithms.md](09-ingest-algorithms.md), which implements Part E1;
> [appendix-b-glossary.md](appendix-b-glossary.md) for the terms in short form.

**Source version:** 1.0, 22 July 2026. Incorporated from `dutch-electricity-explainer.md`.

**Scope.** Electricity only, small connections (kleinverbruik, ≤ 3×80 A). Gas, heat and
large connections are out of scope. Written for someone building or using software that
reasons about household electricity costs, so it is deliberately explicit about the
things that are easy to get wrong.

> **Reliability notice.** Rates below are 2026 values, verified against Rijksoverheid and
> supplier sources in July 2026. **2027 rates are not published.** The regime change on
> 1 January 2027 is law, but the numbers that will apply under it are not yet fixed.
> Anything about 2027 tariffs in this document is structural, not numerical. Treat every
> constant as a variable.

> **A note on the section numbers.** This document's parts are prefixed `E` (E1.1, E5.2,
> Appendix E-A) to keep them distinct from the specification's own `§1.1`, `§5.2` and
> Appendix A. The source document numbered them without the prefix; nothing else about
> the numbering changed.

## Part E1 — What the meter actually measures

### E1.1 The single most important fact

**A Dutch smart meter measures only what crosses the grid connection.**

Electricity generated by your solar panels and consumed immediately in your own house
never passes the meter and is therefore never recorded by it. It is measurable only at
the inverter or at a separate production meter.

This has consequences that surprise people:

- Your meter cannot tell you how much solar you generated.
- Your meter cannot tell you how much electricity your household consumed.
- It can only tell you the *net difference*, direction by direction.

Household consumption must therefore be **reconstructed**:

```
consumption = solar_production + grid_import − grid_export
```

which requires a solar production figure from a second, independent source — the
inverter. Any clock offset or scaling error between those two sources propagates directly
into the reconstructed consumption, and does so invisibly.

> **In the specification:** this is
> [§6.3](09-ingest-algorithms.md#63-household-load-reconstruction), which adds the battery
> terms and the negative-load clamp. The "invisibly" is why
> [§6.17](14-diagnostics.md#617-timestamp-misalignment-detection) exists.

### E1.2 The four registers

A smart meter maintains four cumulative counters, identified by OBIS codes and visible
both on the display and in the P1 telegram:

| OBIS | Meaning | Common name |
|---|---|---|
| `1-0:1.8.1` | Import, tariff 1 | Verbruik T1 |
| `1-0:1.8.2` | Import, tariff 2 | Verbruik T2 |
| `1-0:2.8.1` | Export, tariff 1 | Teruglevering T1 |
| `1-0:2.8.2` | Export, tariff 2 | Teruglevering T2 |

All four are in kWh and all four only ever increase. They reset only on meter replacement.

> **In the specification:** these are the four required `grid_import_t*` /
> `grid_export_t*` series in [§4.1](05-data-formats.md#41-the-series-vocabulary). "Only ever
increase"
> is what [§6.1](09-ingest-algorithms.md#61-cumulative-meter-register--interval-deltas)
> relies on, and meter replacement is the reset it corrects for.

### E1.3 T1 and T2 — do not assume which is which

T1 and T2 are the **normaal** (day/peak) and **dal** (night/off-peak) registers.

The near-universal convention is T1 = normaal and T2 = dal, but **this is a convention,
not a guarantee**, and inverted installations exist. Software should detect the assignment
from the data — whichever register accrues predominantly during night hours is the dal
register — rather than trusting the labels.

The dal window itself is set by the grid operator and **varies by region**. The most
common is 23:00–07:00 on weekdays plus all weekend, but 21:00–07:00 occurs in some areas.
Do not hard-code it.

A subtlety worth internalising: **which registers exist is a property of the meter, not of
the contract.** The meter measures the normaal and dal periods on separate registers, and
that does not change when the household switches supplier or moves between a single-rate
and a dual-rate offer — which Dutch households do freely. A single-rate contract is
applied when the *bill* is computed, by charging both registers at the same rate; it does
not merge them at the meter.

The practical consequence for software: a second register that is absent, or present but
permanently at zero, is evidence of an **incomplete or incorrect installation, or an
incomplete sensor mapping** — not evidence about the household's contract. It should be
surfaced as such rather than silently accepted, and it must not be treated as missing data
that blocks the run.

> **In the specification:**
> [§6.4](09-ingest-algorithms.md#64-tariff-registers--availability-identification-and-use)
> separates register availability, dal identification and tariff-zone pricing, and gates
> the last two on cost simulation.

### E1.4 Internal phase netting — the meter nets across L1, L2, L3

On a three-phase connection, the meter sums the three phases at each measurement instant
and records only the net.

If you export 1,200 W on L1 while a washing machine draws 1,200 W on L2, the meter
registers **nothing at all** — neither import nor export. A Dutch smart meter cannot
register import and export simultaneously; it is one direction or the other.

Two consequences that matter a great deal:

1. **A single-phase inverter or battery on a three-phase connection is not penalised.**
   The phase your generation sits on is financially irrelevant. This is often called
   *intern salderen*, though it is a metering behaviour rather than part of the
   salderingsregeling, and it is **unaffected by the 2027 abolition** — internal phase
   netting continues.

2. **If a meter records both import and export within the same interval, the flow
   reversed in *time*, not across phases.** This makes simultaneous import and export in
   a recorded interval a clean measure of how much sub-interval detail your recording
   resolution has thrown away. On a meter without phase netting the same observation
   would be ambiguous.

The physical caveat: phase netting is financial, not electrical. A single-phase battery
is still limited by the fuse on its own phase, and heavily unbalanced loads still stress
the connection. The meter's indifference to phase allocation does not make the physics
indifferent.

> **In the specification:** consequence 1 is the basis of the soft block in
> [§2.5](03-topology-selector.md#b-battery-phase-configuration) — the financial result is
> close to the 3-phase case. The physical caveat is exactly what the app *cannot* model
> without per-phase data, and is [open question §8.7](17-open-questions.md).
> Consequence 2 is the justification for the overlap diagnostic,
> [§7.1](14-diagnostics.md#71-the-overlap-diagnostic--measure-resolution-damage-directly),
> which reaches the same conclusion from the simulation side.

### E1.5 The P1 port and data resolution

Every Dutch smart meter installed since 2012 exposes a P1 port — an RJ12 serial output
that broadcasts a DSMR telegram. DSMR 5.0 meters emit one per second, DSMR 4.x every ten
seconds. The port is read-only, free to use, and independent of your supplier.

The resolution available to you therefore depends entirely on what you record and retain,
not on the meter:

| Source | Typical resolution | Retention |
|---|---|---|
| P1 port (live) | 1 s / 10 s | Whatever you store |
| Home Assistant short-term statistics | 5 min | ~10 days default |
| Home Assistant long-term statistics | 1 hour | Indefinite |
| Grid operator portal | 15 min or 1 hour | Months to years |
| Supplier invoice | Monthly or annual totals | Contract life |

The practical implication for any long-window analysis is that **you will be working with
hourly data**, because that is what survives. Sub-hourly detail exists only for the recent
past.

Grid operators read the meter remotely — typically monthly for billing, with more granular
data available through their portals — but the P1 port carries no history. It is a live
feed only.

> **In the specification:** this table is the reason
> [§4.3](06-home-assistant-ingestion.md) fetches hourly statistics for the full window and
> 5-minute statistics only for the trailing ten days, and the reason
> [§6.13](14-diagnostics.md#613-resolution-bias-diagnostic) can only ever be computed over
> a short recent window.

## Part E2 — How the bill is constructed

### E2.1 The build-up of a kWh

The price you pay per imported kWh:

```
p_import = (kale_leveringsprijs + inkoopvergoeding + energiebelasting) × (1 + btw)
```

| Component | 2026 value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Kale leveringsprijs | contract-dependent | The bare commodity price |
| Inkoopvergoeding | ~€0.02/kWh | Supplier margin; dynamic contracts only |
| Energiebelasting | €0.09161/kWh excl. VAT | €0.11085 incl. VAT |
| BTW | 21% | Applied to everything, including the energy tax |

A representative all-in import price at a bare price of €0.08/kWh:

```
(0.08 + 0.0205 + 0.09161) × 1.21 ≈ €0.233 /kWh
```

Note the compounding: **VAT is levied on the energy tax as well as on the commodity.**
A €0.01 change in the bare price moves the final price by €0.0121.

> **In the specification:** `import_price()` in
> [§6.5](10-pricing.md#65-price-curves) is this formula exactly.

### E2.2 The energy tax and its bracket structure

Energiebelasting is levied per kWh imported, on a sliding scale where **larger consumers
pay less per unit**. There are five brackets. Households sit entirely within the first
two, which carry identical rates, so for household purposes the tax is effectively flat.

The 2026 electricity rate is €0.09161/kWh excluding VAT, down from €0.1015 in 2025 —
part of a deliberate policy of shifting tax burden from electricity onto gas to encourage
electrification. Gas went the other way, rising to €0.6006/m³ excl. VAT.

**No energy tax is due on electricity you generate and consume yourself.** This is why
self-consumption is worth so much more than export: a self-consumed kWh avoids the
commodity price, the energy tax and the VAT on both.

> **In the specification:** the flat-for-households simplification is why
> `energy_tax_excl_vat` is a single scalar in
> [appendix-a-defaults.md](appendix-a-defaults.md) rather than a bracket table.

### E2.3 Vermindering energiebelasting — the fixed rebate

Every electricity connection to a building with a residential function receives a fixed
annual tax credit, independent of consumption. In 2026 this is **€519.80 excluding VAT
(€628.96 including)**, applied automatically by the supplier.

Two things worth knowing:

- It is a *fixed* amount. It does not scale with consumption, and **nothing you do to
  your consumption changes it.** For any analysis of the effect of a change — a battery,
  a heat pump, more insulation — it cancels out entirely and should be excluded.
- Buildings with one connection serving multiple independent units (a *blokaansluiting*)
  are a known problem area: suppliers commonly apply the rebate once, while the legal
  entitlement may be per independent WOZ object.

### E2.4 Network charges

Netbeheerkosten are paid to the grid operator (Liander, Stedin, Enexis and others), not
to your supplier. You cannot choose your grid operator, and the tariffs are regulated.

The critical structural point for anyone modelling savings: for small connections these
charges are a **capaciteitstarief** — a fixed annual amount determined by the *size* of
your connection, not by how much electricity flows through it. A 1×25 A connection pays
the same network charge whether it draws 500 kWh or 5,000 kWh a year.

Therefore: **a battery does not reduce your network charges.** Nor does solar. Nor does
reducing consumption. Only physically downgrading the connection does, which is rarely
practical and irreversible in effect.

### E2.5 Which components respond to behaviour

This distinction determines what belongs in a savings calculation at all.

| Component | Responds to consumption? | Include in savings analysis? |
|---|---|---|
| Kale leveringsprijs | Yes, per kWh | **Yes** |
| Inkoopvergoeding | Yes, per kWh | **Yes** |
| Energiebelasting | Yes, per kWh | **Yes** |
| BTW | Yes, proportionally | **Yes** |
| Terugleververgoeding | Yes, per exported kWh | **Yes** |
| Terugleverkosten | Yes (2027+, per kWh) | **Yes** |
| Vermindering energiebelasting | No — fixed | No |
| Netbeheerkosten | No — capacity-based | No |
| Vastrecht levering | No — fixed | No |

Including the fixed components in a percentage-saving figure does nothing but dilute it
toward zero and obscures the actual effect. Show them separately if total-bill context is
wanted.

> **In the specification:** this table is the authority for the exclusion list in
> [§6.10](10-pricing.md#610-cost-accounting).

## Part E3 — Contract types

### E3.1 The three forms

**Fixed (vast).** A rate agreed for one to three years, unchanged for the term. Usually
split into normaal and dal rates. Predictable; you carry no market risk and capture no
market opportunity. A battery under a fixed contract can exploit only the day/night
spread — typically 1–3 ct/kWh, which is thin.

**Variable (variabel).** A rate the supplier sets and revises periodically, conventionally
on 1 January and 1 July, though suppliers may change more often. Also usually split
normaal/dal. Between revisions it behaves exactly like a fixed contract.

**Dynamic (dynamisch).** The bare price tracks the EPEX day-ahead market, published a day
in advance. The supplier adds a fixed inkoopvergoeding. Prices can and do go negative
when renewable output is high and demand low — the Netherlands recorded 408 hours of
negative day-ahead prices in the first half of 2025 alone, against 458 hours in all of
2024.

Dynamic contracts are the only ones where a battery has meaningful intraday arbitrage to
capture; daily spreads of 10–20 ct/kWh are common.

> **In the specification:** the three forms are the three branches of
> `bare_supply_price()` in [§6.5](10-pricing.md#65-price-curves) — one code path with
> three rate sources. That the fixed/variable spread is thin is worth remembering when
> reading the charge and discharge bands in
> [§6.6–6.7](11-policies-and-battery.md#66-charge-policy): a band strategy that pays for
> itself on a dynamic contract may do nothing at all on a fixed one.

### E3.2 Settlement granularity — a live and unresolved question

Since **1 October 2025**, EPEX SPOT prices Dutch electricity in **15-minute** blocks
rather than hourly — 96 price points per day instead of 24. This followed the European
CACM regulation and was delayed twice from its original June 2025 date.

**But the market change does not automatically reach your bill.** Several suppliers
announced they would continue billing end customers on hourly prices while handling the
quarter-hourly settlement internally. So two households on nominally similar dynamic
contracts may be billed on entirely different granularities.

This matters more than it sounds:

- If you are billed **hourly**, the hourly average *is* your price. Intra-hour variation
  is invisible to you and you cannot profit from it, however smart your battery.
- If you are billed **quarter-hourly**, intra-hour variation is real money, and analysis
  based on hourly averages will systematically misstate what a battery achieves.

**Check your own contract terms.** This is not inferable from the data, and it is one of
the few parameters where a wrong assumption changes the answer rather than just its
precision. Expect 15-minute settlement to become general over time, but it is not
universal yet.

> **In the specification:** this is the entire justification for `cfg.supplier_settlement`
> and the price bracket in
> [§6.16](14-diagnostics.md#616-price-bracketing-under-settlementresolution-mismatch).
> "Not inferable from the data" is why the app must ask; whether asking or defaulting is
> right is [open question §8.12](17-open-questions.md).

## Part E4 — Salderen: the regime that ends on 31 December 2026

### E4.1 How it works

Under the salderingsregeling, exported and imported kWh are **netted against each other
over the billing year**. Export 2,000 kWh and import 3,000 kWh, and you pay for 1,000 —
at the full retail rate, including energy tax and VAT.

The mechanism is remarkable in its generosity: within the netting allowance, **the grid
functions as a perfectly efficient, cost-free, unlimited battery**. A kWh exported in June
is worth exactly a kWh imported in December, with no losses and no charges.

Export beyond your annual consumption is not netted. That surplus earns a
terugleververgoeding, which is much lower than the retail rate.

### E4.2 Why this matters for battery analysis

**Under salderen, a home battery's solar-shifting function is largely redundant.** The
grid already does the same job, better and for free. This is precisely why residential
battery uptake in the Netherlands lagged behind neighbouring countries, and why it is now
accelerating.

Under salderen a battery is worth having only for:
- intraday price arbitrage on a dynamic contract;
- avoiding terugleverkosten, where the supplier charges them;
- backup power, where the system supports it;
- avoiding inverter shutdown during negative-price or high-voltage periods.

**The implication for anyone modelling historical data is severe.** Data recorded in 2025
or 2026 was generated by a household living under salderen. Simulating that same data
under 2027 rules is legitimate and is usually what you want — but be explicit that you are
answering a counterfactual, and never mix regimes within one *cost* calculation. The
energy answer — how many kWh the battery stores and returns — does not depend on the
regime at all, only on the load, the generation and the policy; it is the euro figure that
is regime-bound.

> **In the specification:** this is why
> [§1.3](01-product-brief.md#13-regulatory-regime--fixed-decision) states the regime as a
> fixed decision rather than a setting, and why the pre-2027 extension point in
> [§6.5](10-pricing.md#65-price-curves) is described as an additional *stage* after the
> flow simulation rather than a per-interval option — a blended regime must not be
> expressible in the cost path. The flow path is unaffected, so a window crossing
> 1 January 2027 is not a special case for the simulation.
>
> The behavioural half of the warning is not modelled and cannot be: a household that
> lived under salderen had no reason to shift load toward its own solar, so its recorded
> load profile is the profile of someone indifferent to self-consumption. A 2027-regime
> household would gradually behave differently. The simulator holds the load profile fixed
> and changes only the prices and the battery, which is the right counterfactual to
> compute and is not the same as a forecast of 2027.

### E4.3 Terugleverkosten today

Since 2024, many suppliers have levied *terugleverkosten* on customers who export
significant volumes. These are typically structured as annual-volume brackets rather than
a flat per-kWh rate, and are usually zero on dynamic contracts.

They have been contentious. The regulator ACM found that some customers exporting more
than they consume ended up **paying, net, to export**. Because the 2016 model contract had
no component for such a charge, suppliers levied it through other lines on the bill — the
vastrecht in particular — which the ACM characterised as a disguised charge and has moved
to correct by adding an explicit component to the model contract.

> **In the specification:** the annual-volume bracket shape is why `tlk_mode = TIERED`
> exists at all in [§6.5](10-pricing.md#65-price-curves), even though `FLAT` is the
> expected 2027 shape.

## Part E5 — The regime from 1 January 2027

### E5.1 What the law does

The *Wet beëindiging salderingsregeling* ends net metering outright on 1 January 2027.
There is no phase-down; the mechanism stops. Exported electricity can no longer be offset
against consumption in any part or form.

Instead, every exported kWh earns a **redelijke vergoeding** — a reasonable compensation
— from the supplier, and every imported kWh is charged in full.

### E5.2 The rules on compensation

Four provisions, several added by amendment during passage, define the shape:

1. **Minimum 50%.** Between 1 January 2027 and 1 January 2030, the compensation must be
   at least half the bare supply price — the commodity price excluding taxes.

2. **Never negative.** The compensation may not be a negative amount. Importantly, an
   amendment specified this is assessed **over a period of at least one month**, not
   instant by instant. Individual negative-price intervals do not breach it as long as the
   monthly aggregate is non-negative.

3. **ACM supervision, with teeth.** The regulator oversees compensation levels and may
   intervene against unreasonable rates; a further amendment gave it power to set a
   minimum compensation **retroactively**.

4. **Costs restricted.** Charges that are discriminatory, or not directly or indirectly
   related to feed-in, are prohibited for households and micro-enterprises. Feed-in costs
   may be charged only to *active customers* — the people who actually cause them — rather
   than spread across all customers.

> **In the specification:** provision 1 is `feedin_alpha = 0.50`. Provision 2 is the
> `feedin_floor_mode` treatment in [§6.5](10-pricing.md#65-price-curves) — note that the
> assessment period is "at least one month", so a supplier may legally use a longer one;
> see [open question §8.14](17-open-questions.md). Provision 4 constrains which
> terugleverkosten preset shapes are plausible, which bears on
> [open question §8.5](17-open-questions.md).

### E5.3 Terugleverkosten continue

Feed-in charges do not disappear in 2027. The ACM expects them to remain necessary,
because part of the extra cost of handling exported solar persists, and the law's own
provisions prevent those costs from being fully absorbed into the compensation. The
regulator has moved to include them explicitly in the model contracts.

So the arithmetic per exported kWh is:

```
net_received = redelijke_vergoeding − terugleverkosten
```

and while the compensation itself may not be negative, **the net after charges can be**.

The ACM has been notably blunt in framing expectations: exported solar does not suddenly
become more lucrative after salderen ends. Its market value stays modest, while the extra
costs from profile and imbalance differences persist.

### E5.4 What it means numerically

Illustrative, at a bare price of €0.08/kWh:

| | Under salderen (≤2026) | From 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of importing 1 kWh | €0.233 | €0.233 |
| Value of exporting 1 kWh | €0.233 (netted) | €0.04 – €0.09 |
| Ratio | 1 : 1 | roughly 3 : 1 |

The gap between what a kWh costs to buy and what it earns to sell goes from **zero to
roughly 14–19 ct/kWh**, depending on where the compensation lands within its plausible
range. That gap is the entire economic case for a home battery, and its width is the
reason the uncertainty in Part E7 matters: at the optimistic end of feed-in compensation
the case is materially weaker than at the pessimistic end.

It also reverses the priority order for what to do with surplus solar:

1. **Consume it immediately** — avoids the full retail price. Best.
2. **Store it and consume it later** — avoids the full retail price, less round-trip
   losses. Nearly as good.
3. **Export it** — earns roughly a third of retail, possibly less after charges. Worst.

Under salderen, options 1 and 3 were financially identical. From 2027 they differ by a
factor of three.

> **In the specification:** this priority order is exactly what the charge and discharge
> policies encode — PV surplus taken first, house discharge before grid export, and
> `allow_grid_export` defaulting off
> ([§6.6–6.7](11-policies-and-battery.md#66-charge-policy)).

## Part E6 — Implications worth stating plainly

### E6.1 Discharging to the house beats exporting, by a lot

A kWh discharged to serve household load avoids paying `p_import` — around €0.23. A kWh
exported to the grid earns `p_export` — perhaps €0.06. Home discharge is worth roughly
**three to four times** as much.

Grid arbitrage — buying cheap to sell dear — has to clear a wide spread to be worth
anything: you buy at the full retail price *including energy tax and VAT*, and sell at a
fraction of the bare price with no tax component at all. In practice it only works when
you charge at deeply negative prices. Battery marketing rarely makes this distinction, and
it is the single most common source of over-optimistic savings estimates.

### E6.2 Negative prices invert the usual logic

When the spot price goes negative and your net feed-in charge exceeds your compensation,
**exporting costs you money**. In those intervals a battery's value is not in arbitrage
but simply in *not exporting* — soaking up solar that would otherwise be sold at a loss.

This is also why some inverters shut down during negative-price periods, and why a battery
that can absorb generation instead has value beyond the price spread itself.

> **In the specification:** the negative `p_export_net` case is explicit in
> [§6.10](10-pricing.md#610-cost-accounting), and the optional `economic_guard` in
> [§6.7](11-policies-and-battery.md#67-discharge-policy) exists for it — defaulting off,
> because policies stay literal.

### E6.3 The tax asymmetry is the whole game

Energy tax and VAT are levied on import and not on export. That asymmetry — not the
commodity price — is what makes self-consumption valuable. Roughly €0.11 of every imported
kWh is tax, and it vanishes entirely if you never import that kWh.

This is why a battery's value depends much more on the *tax regime* than on the *battery*.
A change in energy tax policy moves battery economics more than a change in cell chemistry.

> **In the specification:** this is why the tax and tariff constants must be editable and
> are stamped with their year ([appendix-a-defaults.md](appendix-a-defaults.md)). A result
> computed with 2026 constants is a result about the 2026 tax regime.

### E6.4 Fixed charges do not move, so do not count them

Network charges are capacity-based and the tax rebate is a flat amount. Neither responds
to a battery. Including them in a savings percentage understates the effect on the part of
the bill that actually changed.

## Part E7 — What is not yet known

Stated plainly, because confident-sounding 2027 numbers are circulating and most of them
are estimates.

| Question | Status as of July 2026 |
|---|---|
| 2027 energy tax rate | **Not published.** Set annually in the Belastingplan. |
| 2027 vermindering energiebelasting | **Not published.** |
| Actual terugleververgoeding levels | **Unknown.** Only the 50% floor is law. Suppliers have not published 2027 rates. |
| Terugleverkosten levels from 2027 | **Unknown.** Expected to fall, but persist. |
| How compensation is defined for dynamic contracts | **Ambiguous.** "50% of the supply price" is clear for a fixed rate; for an hourly-varying price, whether the reference is the spot price alone or spot plus supplier margin is not settled. |
| Which suppliers will bill quarter-hourly | **Partially known.** Varies by supplier; check your own contract. |
| Whether a 2030 review changes the regime | The law is scheduled for evaluation; an impact assessment after three years was added by amendment. |

Figures in the €0.03–0.09/kWh range for 2027 compensation and charges, which appear
widely on comparison sites, are **projections**. They are reasonable, and they are not
tariffs.

> **In the specification:** row 5 of this table is
> [open question §8.1](17-open-questions.md), stated there in the form the code has to
> resolve it. Row 6 is [§8.12](17-open-questions.md). The unpublished-tariff rows are why
> `tlk_eur_per_kwh` is labelled a placeholder and why
> [§8.5](17-open-questions.md) argues against supplier-named presets.

## Appendix E-B — Formulas

```
# Import, all contract types, 2027+
p_import(t) = (bare(t) + inkoopvergoeding + energiebelasting) × (1 + btw)

  where bare(t) =
    dynamic  : EPEX spot at t (hourly or quarter-hourly per contract)
    fixed    : constant, possibly split normaal/dal
    variable : piecewise constant, possibly split normaal/dal

# Export, 2027+
compensation(t) = max(0, α × bare(t) + β)     with α ≥ 0.5 until 2030
p_export(t)     = compensation(t) − terugleverkosten
                  # may be negative; the ≥0 constraint applies to
                  # compensation, assessed over ≥1 month, not to the net

# Export, ≤2026 (salderen)
  netted against import up to annual consumption, at p_import
  surplus beyond that at the terugleververgoeding

# Household consumption (never measured directly)
consumption = solar_production + grid_import − grid_export

# Excluded from any savings analysis — these do not respond to behaviour
  netbeheerkosten (capacity-based)
  vastrecht levering (fixed)
  vermindering energiebelasting (fixed)
```

> **Note on the export formula.** The `max(0, …)` written per interval above is a
> shorthand; the comment beneath it carries the actual rule, which is assessed over ≥1
> month. [§6.5](10-pricing.md#65-price-curves) implements the monthly form and explains
> why the two are not equivalent — the per-interval clamp is the more generous of the
> two, not the stricter.

## Appendix E-C — Glossary

| Dutch | English | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Salderingsregeling | Net metering scheme | Annual netting of export against import. Ends 1 Jan 2027. |
| Intern salderen | Internal phase netting | Meter sums the three phases. Unaffected by 2027. |
| Kale leveringsprijs | Bare supply price | Commodity price, excl. tax and VAT. |
| Inkoopvergoeding | Purchase fee | Supplier margin per kWh on dynamic contracts. |
| Energiebelasting | Energy tax | Per-kWh tax on import. Not levied on export. |
| Vermindering energiebelasting | Energy tax rebate | Fixed annual credit per connection. |
| Terugleververgoeding | Feed-in compensation | Paid per exported kWh. |
| Redelijke vergoeding | Reasonable compensation | The 2027+ legal term for the above. |
| Terugleverkosten | Feed-in charges | Levied per exported kWh by the supplier. |
| Netbeheerkosten | Network charges | Paid to the grid operator. Capacity-based. |
| Capaciteitstarief | Capacity tariff | Network charge by connection size, not volume. |
| Vastrecht | Standing charge | Fixed supplier fee. |
| Normaal / dal | Peak / off-peak | Day and night tariff periods. |
| Telwerk | Register | One of the meter's four counters. |
| Kleinverbruiker | Small consumer | Connection ≤ 3×80 A. |
| Actieve afnemer | Active customer | Customer who also feeds in. |
| Slimme meter | Smart meter | Remotely read meter with a P1 port. |
| DSMR | — | Dutch Smart Meter Requirements, the P1 telegram standard. |
